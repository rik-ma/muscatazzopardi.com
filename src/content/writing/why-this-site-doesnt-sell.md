There is no services page on this site. No testimonials, no client logos, no case study with a tidy before-and-after, no button that books a call. Since I spend a good part of my working week telling other firms what belongs on their websites, I owe you a defence of the empty space.

The lens I used is one I borrowed. In *The Trusted Advisor* (2000), David Maister, Charles Green and Robert Galford proposed an equation for how much someone trusts you:

> Trust = (Credibility + Reliability + Intimacy) ÷ Self-Orientation

Three things build trust. One thing divides whatever the other three have managed to build, and that one sits underneath everything else in the fraction, which turns out to matter a great deal. When a firm asks me to look at their website, I go page by page and ask which term each page is working on, and for whose benefit. It is a blunt instrument, and it is rude about most homepages. Before building this one, I ran the same test on every page I was tempted to include, and this essay is the audit trail.

Credibility first, since it is the term websites handle best. Here it lives in the writing. The essays are positions I can defend, mostly learned from things I got wrong at least once before I got them right. The playbooks go further: they are the same methods I use in paid work, written up in enough detail that you can run them on your own firm without me in the room. Publishing the method beats describing it. Anyone can claim a process; showing the whole thing costs something, and the cost is what makes the claim believable. That is the educational bet of the whole site. If the methods are any good, they should be good in your hands too.

Reliability is harder for a website, because a site is a photograph and reliability is a habit. Two small mechanisms carry it here. The now page says what I am working on and carries the date it was last touched; I update it quarterly, so the date makes the promise checkable by anyone. The newsletter only goes out when I have written something. There is no welcome sequence, no drip campaign warming you toward a pitch. Some months you will get two essays. Some months, silence. Neither mechanism is impressive, and that is deliberate: reliability is a record of small promises kept, and you can only keep the promises you were careful about making.

Intimacy is the term that makes consultants squirm, and the fixes here are modest. One photograph of me, and only one. First person all the way through, because a third-person bio is a strange thing to write about yourself and a stranger thing to believe. Gozo shows up in these pages because I live there, with my wife Sarah and a house that is going up slowly, and pretending the work happens in some placeless professional nowhere would make every other page a little less true.

Then the denominator. Self-orientation is how much of the encounter is about you, the seller, and it divides everything above the line. This is where the missing pages went. A services page is written for its writer; it sorts visitors into buyers and non-buyers at the door. Testimonials are praise curated by the person being praised. Case studies carry the same problem with an extra one attached: the work in them belongs to my clients, and their stories are not mine to spend. A booking funnel treats a reader as pipeline before they have said a word. Every one of those raises the denominator, and when the denominator grows, the equation only moves one way.

The standard advice at this point is moderation. Keep a services page but make it tasteful, use three testimonials instead of twelve, soften the funnel. I do not buy it, because toning the apparatus down does not change what the apparatus is for; a quieter sales page is still written for the seller. So the apparatus is not here at all. The absence is the strategy. There was never a services page to make tasteful, and leaving the room out of the building does more for the denominator than any amount of restraint inside it.

I can hear the objection, mostly because I have raised it against other people: a site that refuses to sell is still positioning, and refusing loudly can become its own kind of pitch. (Performing selflessness has a self-orientation all of its own. I am not claiming immunity.) The claim I will actually make is smaller. Everything here works whether or not we ever speak. The playbooks run without me, the essays argue without me, and if you take a method from these pages, use it on your firm, and never send me a word, the site has done exactly what it was built to do.

Which leaves one loose end: whether any of this holds over time. That part is checkable. The now page has a date at the top. If it is more than a quarter old, the reliability term has slipped, and you should discount the rest of this essay accordingly.
